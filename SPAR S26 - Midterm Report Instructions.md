# SPAR Midterm Report Instructions

The midterm report is due on April 3rd by default, but we’ll accept late submissions (please let us know). This outline is here to help you write a useful midterm report. However, feel free to deviate from this structure if need be\! We just ask that you write some sort of abstract and give preliminary results and methods that you used so far. 

### **Format and medium**

You’re welcome to use any tool you like, though we recommend LaTeX as a default for any work that’s likely to turn into a journal publication, as it makes it easy to turn into a paper and is often a requirement for journals. If you’re using LaTeX, we recommend using [Overleaf](https://www.overleaf.com/), which significantly simplifies collaboration and [our LaTeX template](https://github.com/the-kairos-project/spar-report-template). If you’re using our template, remember to switch to \\`documentclass[midterm]{sparreport}`. 

If you don’t need a LaTeX source, we recommend [Typst](https://typst.app/) for a professional-looking PDF, [Quarto](https://quarto.org/) for publishing as a website, or Google Docs for ease of use or targeting a forum like LessWrong.

State the main research problem. What precisely are you studying, and why is it important? Will it fill a gap in other studies? Try to keep this section to 65*–*155 words.

Discuss your research methodology. Describe the specific design of the study and provide clear and concise description of the procedures that were performed. Try to keep this section to 90*–*300 words.

## Discussion

This section will also be highly individualized and we encourage groups to break up this section with subheadings to make it organized and readable.

This section is where you can include analysis and discussion of the quantitative results from above. Here’s where you would include hypotheses about what could be driving your results, interesting future lines of research, and potential implications of your results on other research agendas. ## Limitations of Study (optional)

Indicate what you intend to do, and what you are not going to attempt. You may limit the scope of your paper by any number of factors, for example, time, resources, ability, etc.

## Literature Review (optional)

This section reviews relevant research and previous studies to identify knowledge gaps and uncover new insights on the topic. A strong literature review broadens the study's context and helps readers grasp related works more easily.

## Conclusion

The conclusion summarizes the paper's main points and restates the research question or hypothesis. It highlights the research's major contributions without adding new information or repeating previous details. Try to keep this section to 45*–*110 words. 

## Works Cited/Bibliography

The reference page lists all sources used for your project, including those cited in the review and methods sections. Ensure the citation style is consistent throughout.

# ⚒️ Empirical Research Outline

# SPAR Mid-term Report Outline: Empirical Research

*This outline is for projects involving running experiments, training models, or collecting quantitative data.*

**Total target: 500** *–* **1,000 words**

---

# \[Title of the project\]

### \[All contributors’ names\]

\[All contributors’ emails in corresponding order to the names section\]

## Abstract

*45–90 words*

Summarize your research question, approach, key results, and main takeaway in one paragraph. Someone should be able to read just this and understand what you did and why it matters. Don’t add citations here.

## Introduction

*90–175 words*

What problem are you addressing, and why does it matter for reducing risks from advanced AI? State your research question or hypothesis clearly. End with a brief walk through of your contributions.

## Related Work

*65–130 words*

Position your work relative to existing research. What prior work does your project build on? What gap does it fill? You don't need to be exhaustive: focus on the 3–5 most relevant papers or lines of work.

## Methods

*110–200 words*

Describe your experimental setup clearly enough that someone could replicate it. Include: model architectures, datasets, training procedures, evaluation metrics, and any key implementation choices. If you're using existing tools or frameworks, cite them.

## Results

*155–265 words*

Present your findings. Lead with your main results, then add supporting details. Use figures, tables, and graphs when they add clarity. A well-designed figure will often communicate more than several paragraphs. Make sure to include any negative results as well.

## Discussion

*110–175 words*

Interpret your results. What do they mean for your original question? Were there surprises? What are the limitations of your approach? What would you do differently with more time? Suggest 2–3 concrete directions for future work.

## Conclusion

*45–90 words*

Briefly restate your main contribution and its significance. Don’t add any new information here, just write a clean summary of the contributions made.

## References

Use a consistent citation format throughout. We recommend using IEEE citation style, or the format of your target venue.

# 💡 Conceptual Research Outline

# SPAR Mid-term Report Outline: Conceptual & Theoretical Research

*This outline is for projects developing frameworks, definitions, threat models, or philosophical arguments rather than running research experiments.*

**Total target: 500** *–* **1,000 words**

---

# \[Title of the project\]

### \[All contributors’ names\]

\[All contributors’ emails in corresponding order to the names section\]

## Abstract

*45–90 words*

Summarize the problem you're addressing, your approach or thesis, and your main conclusions. Make the stakes clear. Why should someone working in AI safety care about this?

## Introduction

*90–175 words*

Motivate the problem. Why is this question important, and why is it hard or underexplored? State your thesis or main claim clearly. Walk the reader through your argumentative strategy.

## Background & Setup

*90–175 words*

Define key terms and introduce any frameworks, assumptions, or prior work you're building on. The goal is to get the reader oriented before your main argument. If you're responding to or critiquing existing work, summarize it fairly here.

## Main Argument

*220–400 words*

This is the core of your report. Develop your argument in a logical structure, using subsections as needed. Common patterns:

* **Claim → Evidence → Implications** (for a single main thesis)  
* **Problem → Analysis → Proposed Framework** (for definitional/taxonomic work)  
* **Position A → Position B → Synthesis** (for comparative or dialectical work)

Be explicit about your reasoning. Flag where you're making assumptions or where you think your argument is weakest.

## Implications & Future Directions

*90–155 words*

So what? If your argument is correct, what follows for AI safety research or practice? What questions remain open? What would it take to further validate or refute your conclusions?

## Conclusion

*45–90 words*

Restate your main claim and why it’s important in 1–2 paragraphs.

## References

Conceptual work will often engage heavily with the existing literature. Cite generously and accurately. We recommend using the APA citation style by default.

## Executive Summary

*65–110 words*

A standalone summary aimed at a busy reader (think: a policymaker or someone in your target audience). State the problem, your key findings, and your top 2 or 3 recommendations. This should be understandable without reading the rest of the report.

## Introduction & Problem Statement

*90–150 words*

What policy problem or governance gap are you addressing? Why is it important now? Who are the relevant actors and stakeholders? Be specific about scope: which jurisdictions, technologies, or institutions are you focusing on?

## Background & Context

*90–155 words*

Provide necessary context: relevant legal frameworks, the institutional environment, recent developments, or prior policy precedent. Assume your reader is familiar with risks from AI broadly but not with the specific policy area, unless this report is directly aimed at non-expert policy audiences.

## Analysis

*175–310 words*

This is your main contribution. Depending on your project, this could include:

* Comparative analysis of policy options  
* Case studies of existing governance mechanisms  
* Stakeholder analysis or political feasibility assessment  
* Gap analysis of current frameworks

Use evidence and cite sources. Be explicit about your analytical framework and any normative assumptions you're making.

## Recommendations

*110–200 words*

What should be done, and by whom? Be concrete and action-oriented. For each recommendation, briefly address:

* Why this action (link back to your analysis)  
* Who should take it (be specific about actors)  
* Key considerations or tradeoffs

It's fine to distinguish between “ideal” and “feasible” recommendations, or to offer alternatives for different scenarios.

## Conclusion

*45–90 words*

Summarize the problem, your analysis, and any next steps. End with a clear statement of why this matters.

## References

Cite primary sources (legislation, official reports, statements) where possible. We recommend using the APA citation style by default.
